1

Water on the floor, a stain overhead, or a meter that will not stop

Most leak calls begin with a symptom, not a diagnosis. The bill doubled, a ceiling turned brown, the floor feels damp near a wall, or the meter dial keeps creeping when every tap is off.

Each of those has a short list of likely causes, and leak detection is how we tell which one you have. We test first, then recommend a repair, so you pay to fix the actual pipe and not a guess.

2

When a hidden leak is an emergency

Call the 24/7 line right away if water is running through a ceiling, pooling near electrical outlets or a panel, soaking a floor, or if the meter is moving fast with nothing in use. Water follows wiring and framing, and a small leak overnight can become a soaked ceiling by morning.

If you can reach the main shutoff, close it and open one low faucet to relieve pressure. We take after-hours, weekend and holiday calls because a leak does not wait for a business day. A pipe that has plainly split belongs on our burst pipe repair page.

3

What to do while you wait

These steps limit damage and make the diagnosis faster.

  1. 1
    Close the main shutoffTurn it clockwise and open one low faucet to drain the lines if water is actively running.
  2. 2
    Protect electronics and outletsMove devices away from wet areas and do not touch outlets near standing water. If water reaches a panel, stay away and call 911.
  3. 3
    Photograph everythingTake dated photos of stains, the meter reading and the damage. They help the plumber and any insurance claim.
  4. 4
    Note the meter numberWrite down the reading and the time so we can repeat the flow test and compare.
  5. 5
    Clear accessMove stored items from closets, the water heater area and crawlspace doors so the plumber can reach the pipes.

Do not cut into drywall yourself unless water is trapped and bulging, because it makes the leak harder to trace. Pressure testing follows the California Plumbing Code (UPC based), which is the reference we use for test pressures and acceptable pipe materials.

4

Meter still running or a high water bill

A bill that jumps with no change in how many people live in the home is the classic sign of a hidden leak. You can run the first check yourself. Turn off every fixture, the ice maker and the irrigation, then look at the leak indicator on the meter, usually a small triangle or dial. If it turns, water is leaving the house somewhere.

The meter flow test, step by step

Write down the meter reading, keep all water off for 30 to 60 minutes, then read it again. A changed number means a leak. Next, close the main shutoff at the house. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house plumbing. If it keeps running, the leak is on the buried line between the meter and the house.

That one test cuts the search in half before any instrument is used. It also gives you something concrete to tell the plumber on the phone.

Silent leaks that mimic a leak

A running toilet flapper wastes more water than most people expect and makes almost no noise. Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and see whether color reaches the bowl. Dripping faucets, a stuck pressure relief valve on the water heater and a failing softener valve are other quiet culprits we check first.

5

Wet ceiling, damp wall or a stain that keeps growing

A ceiling stain is where the water ended up, not always where it started. Water can travel along a joist or pipe for several feet before it soaks drywall, so cutting a hole directly under the stain often misses the source. We use a moisture meter to map the wet area and a thermal imaging camera to see temperature differences behind the surface.

Not every stain is a supply leak. The source could be a shower pan, a bathroom above, a drain line, a roof or window leak, or condensation on a cold pipe. A pressure test of the supply lines and a fill test of the drain tell us which system is responsible, and that decides whether a plumber or a roofer is the right call.

6

Leaks under the house or in the crawlspace

Older raised-foundation homes have a crawlspace where pipes hang under the floor. A leak there can go unnoticed for months, showing up as a musty smell, soft flooring, a persistent damp patch in a closet, or a meter that never fully rests. Wet insulation and mineral stains on a pipe are visual clues, and pressure gauges on the lines help confirm.

We check supply lines, valves, water heater connections and drain joints from below. If the crawlspace is flooded or access is unsafe, we say so and wait before anyone goes under.

7

Underground and buried line leaks

When the meter keeps running even after the house shutoff is closed, the leak is outside, on the service line from the meter to the house. Signs include a soggy patch of lawn or driveway on a dry day, a patch of unusually green grass, low pressure at every tap, or a hissing sound near the meter box.

This is where acoustic ground microphones earn their cost. The pipe is isolated, the microphone listens along the surface, and the sound of escaping water is loudest at the leak. We confirm with a second reading before anyone digs. If the line must be replaced, our water line repair and replacement page explains trench and trenchless options.

Leak-finding methods compared

Different leaks call for different tools. Here is how the main methods fit the situation, so you know why a plumber chooses one over another.

MethodWhat it detectsBest forLimits
Meter flow testWhether water is leaving the propertyFirst check on a high billDoes not find the location
Acoustic ground microphonesSound of pressurized water escapingBuried service lines, slab leaksLoud traffic and plastic pipe reduce accuracy
Tracer gasLight gas escaping from a sealed, emptied lineHard-to-hear leaks, plastic or deep pipesLine must be drained and sealed first
Thermal imagingTemperature differences on walls, ceilings, floorsHot water leaks, radiant lines, stainsShows a surface pattern, not an exact point
Pressure testingPressure drop on an isolated lineConfirming which line is leakingNarrows the section but not the spot

Real jobs usually combine two or three of these. The flow test narrows the zone, pressure testing picks the line, and a listening or gas method pinpoints the spot.

10

Sprinklers, pool and spa, and the meter box

Irrigation is a frequent hidden water user. A cracked sprinkler riser, a valve that sticks open or a broken drip line can waste water for weeks and sit outside the house loop. Turn the controller off and watch the meter, and if it stops, the sprinkler system is the source. We can isolate the zone valves and test each branch.

Pools and spas lose water to evaporation, so a bucket test helps separate evaporation from a leak. We cover the plumbing side: fill line, return plumbing and equipment connections. A shell or liner leak is a job for a pool specialist.

The meter box deserves a look too. A weeping shutoff or corroded connection can leave the box wet in dry weather. If the leak is on the utility side of the meter, call the City of Huntington Park Water and Sewer Division.

11

What a leak detection visit looks like

The process is the same whether the call comes at 2 p.m. or 2 a.m., only the urgency changes.

  1. 1
    Talk through the symptomsWe ask when you first noticed it, where the water shows up and whether the bill changed, then check the meter together.
  2. 2
    Isolate the systemWe shut off by zone, test the supply side and the drain side separately, and rule out toilets, irrigation and appliances.
  3. 3
    Pinpoint with instrumentsAcoustic microphones, thermal imaging, a moisture meter or tracer gas narrow the leak to a small spot.
  4. 4
    Explain the repair optionsYou see the location, what the repair involves and the price before any opening is cut.
  5. 5
    Repair or hand offWe fix the pipe, patch the access and recheck the meter. If the problem is a roof or a utility-side line, we tell you who to call.
12

Worth knowing before you pay for a dig

A good detection report names the leak location in plain terms and shows how it was confirmed. Be cautious about any plumber who proposes opening a wall or breaking concrete without testing first. Insurance rules differ by policy, and many policies treat sudden damage differently from slow seepage, so call your insurer early and keep photos, the bill and the detection findings.
