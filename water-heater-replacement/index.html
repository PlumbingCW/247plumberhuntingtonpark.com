1

A failed tank is a flood waiting to happen

Once the steel shell of a tank corrodes through, it does not heal. Seepage becomes a steady stream, and a heater in a garage or closet can dump forty to fifty gallons across the floor in one go. That is the situation where waiting until Monday costs more than acting tonight.

If your heater is leaking from the body, close the cold water valve above it, switch off the breaker or gas control, and call. We handle after-hours replacements, and we will tell you what can be done the same day and what must wait for a part.

4

Tank, tankless or heat pump: choosing the replacement type

A standard tank is the lowest upfront cost and the easiest swap, because the piping and venting are already set up for it. A tankless unit heats water on demand and can save floor space, but it often needs a larger gas line, new venting and sometimes an upgraded electrical circuit. We cover that in detail on the tankless water heater page.

A heat pump (hybrid) water heater pulls heat from surrounding air, so it uses far less electricity than a standard electric tank. It needs room around it, a condensate drain and enough air volume, so it suits garages and larger utility closets better than tight hallway cabinets. Ask us to check your space before you choose, since the right type depends on where it will sit.

6

California bracing and the permit question

California Health and Safety Code section 19211 requires new and replacement water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped against earthquake motion, and the installation must follow the California Plumbing Code (UPC based) or any local changes to it. We strap every replacement tank to the framing or wall, and we flag older heaters that are not secured.

Permit rules for water heater replacement vary, so ask the City of Huntington Park building department before work starts. We can describe the work in the terms the department uses, but we do not guess at fees or inspection requirements. Pulling a permit when one is needed protects you at resale and with insurance.

What the replacement visit looks like

A typical swap takes a few hours when the new tank is ready, longer for a fuel change or tankless conversion.

  1. 1
    Shutoff and drainWe close the supply, switch off power or gas, and drain the old tank to a safe point outside or to a floor drain.
  2. 2
    Disconnect and removeWe disconnect the lines, vent and relief pipe, and carry the old unit out for disposal.
  3. 3
    Set the new unitWe place the heater on a drain pan where required, level it and connect new flexible lines with shutoffs.
  4. 4
    Valves, venting and expansionWe install a new temperature and pressure relief valve and discharge pipe, check the vent, and add an expansion tank if your system needs one.
  5. 5
    Brace and testWe strap the tank, fill it, purge air, check for leaks, test gas connections or electrical continuity, and light or power it.

We walk you through the temperature setting and where the shutoff valves are before we leave.

9

Hard water and the life of your next tank

Local groundwater averages 214 mg/L hardness per the city's report, and imported MWD water averages 235 mg/L. Both sit above the USGS 180 mg/L line for very hard water. In practice, that means scale piling up inside your tank and shortening its life.

You can protect the new heater with a yearly flush and an anode rod check, and you can cut the scale at the source with a softener, as described on our water softener and filtration page. If your last tank lasted fewer years than expected, hard water is a likely reason.

10

Mistakes to avoid when replacing a heater

Do not pick a heater by price alone, because a cheaper tank with a shorter warranty can cost more over ten years. Do not skip the expansion tank if your home has a closed system, and do not let anyone leave the old relief valve line in place. Ask for a licensed plumber and check the license on the state CSLB website.
